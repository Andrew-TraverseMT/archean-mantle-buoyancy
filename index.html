How melting made Archean mantle lithosphere buoyant

Partial melting strips iron, garnet, and clinopyroxene from peridotite. The residue is less dense than fertile mantle, so a cold lithospheric column can stay afloat. That trick worked best in the Archean, when the mantle was hotter, melting went further, and the leftover rock was more depleted. Losing H2O and CO2 matters far less for density than the melt itself.

What the sliders are showing

Fertile peridotite is a mix of olivine, orthopyroxene, clinopyroxene, and an Al-phase (spinel or garnet). Melt prefers iron, calcium, and aluminum, so the leftover rock has a higher Mg# and less of the dense minerals garnet and clinopyroxene. That is compositional buoyancy: the residue is intrinsically lighter than the asthenosphere it came from, even before you worry about temperature (Jordan 1978, 1988).

The Archean is the important case because ambient mantle potential temperature was about 150–250 °C higher than today (Herzberg, Condie & Korenaga 2010). For the same lithosphere thickness, melting started deeper and went further — typically ~30% melt and 25–35 km of mafic crust, versus ~10% melt and ~7 km of modern MORB crust. The Archean residue is a high-Mg# harzburgite; a modern residue of the same thickness is still closer to lherzolite.

Devolatilization is on the page so you can see that it is secondary. Water in olivine expands the lattice a little (Smyth et al. 2006), and a few percent phlogopite or magnesite is lighter than peridotite, so drying the rock makes it slightly denser, not lighter. Peslier et al. (2010) argued that the real job of a dry cratonic root is viscosity, not buoyancy. Move the volatile slider across its full range: the density change stays small next to 20–30% melt extraction.

Density versus melt fraction follows Schutt & Lesher (2006) at 4 GPa, where they report −1.14% at 20% melt — the largest depletion effect in their 1–7 GPa set. Lee (2003) fitted xenolith densities to Mg# instead: ρ = −0.0144 Mg# + 4.66. The two curves disagree in detail (Schutt & Lesher find a weaker Fe-loss effect than Jordan 1979 and a stronger role for mineral modes). This page uses Schutt & Lesher because the control is melt fraction. The Lee curve is plotted as a dashed alternative and is not used for the verdict.

Sources used in the calculation

  1. Jordan, T. H. (1978). Composition and development of the continental tectosphere. Nature 274, 544–548. Also Jordan (1988), Journal of Petrology, Special Lithosphere Issue.
  2. Lee, C.-T. A. (2003). Compositional variation of density and seismic velocities in natural peridotites at STP conditions. JGR 108, 2441. Table 5: ρ = −0.0144 Mg# + 4.66.
  3. Schutt, D. L., & Lesher, C. E. (2006). Effects of melt depletion on the density and seismic velocity of garnet and spinel lherzolite. JGR 111, B05401. Primary Δρ(F) used here, 4 GPa tabulated value.
  4. Katz, R. F., Spiegelman, M., & Langmuir, C. H. (2003). A new parameterization of hydrous mantle melting. G-Cubed 4, 1073. Melt fraction, including the 2023 errata for temperature units and Rcpx.
  5. Herzberg, C., Condie, K., & Korenaga, J. (2010). Thermal history of the Earth and its petrological expression. EPSL 292, 79–88. Modern TP ≈ 1350 °C; Archean ambient ≈ 1500–1600 °C; ~30% Archean melting.
  6. Walter, M. J. (1998). Melting of garnet peridotite and the origin of komatiite and depleted lithosphere. Journal of Petrology 39, 29–60. Fertile KR4003 Mg# = 89.2.
  7. Bernstein, S., Kelemen, P. B., & Hanghøj, K. (2007). Consistent olivine Mg# in cratonic mantle reflects Archean melting to orthopyroxene exhaustion. Geology 35, 459–462. Mg# ≈ 92.8 at F ≈ 0.40.
  8. Afonso, J. C., & Schutt, D. L. (2012). The effects of polybaric partial melting on density and seismic velocities of mantle restites. Lithos. Mg# rises nearly linearly with F.
  9. Smyth, J. R., Frost, D. J., Nestola, F., Holl, C. M., & Bromiley, G. (2006). Olivine hydration in the deep upper mantle. GRL 33, L15301. 5000 ppm H2O ≡ ~240 °C of thermal expansion (Fei 1995).
  10. Eeken, T., et al. (2018). Seismic evidence for a mid-lithospheric discontinuity in cratons. Hydrous-mineral mode scaling: 0.25 wt% H2O ≈ 13% hydrous assemblage.
  11. Hirth, G., & Kohlstedt, D. L. (1996). Water in the oceanic upper mantle. Ambient ~125 ± 75 ppm H2O.
  12. Dasgupta, R., & Hirschmann, M. M. (2010). The deep carbon cycle and melting in Earth’s interior. EPSL. DMM-like CO2 of order 102 ppm.
  13. Peslier, A. H., Woodland, A. B., Bell, D. R., & Lazarov, M. (2010). Olivine water contents in the continental lithosphere and the longevity of cratons. Nature 467, 78–81. Dry olivine as a viscosity, not density, argument.
